Signals of Smartphone Addiction

Skills

Tools

D3.js · JavaScript · HTML · CSS

Team

Nia Ivanova and Cecilia Chen

Project Overview

This project explored how smartphone usage patterns can indicate potentially addictive behaviour. Working with a dataset on screen time, sleep, stress, notifications, app opens, social media use and addiction level, we designed an interactive D3 dashboard around one main question: what patterns of smartphone usage indicate potentially addictive behaviour when considering multiple indicators such as usage habits, sleep duration, and stress levels?

The final dashboard combined three views. A heatmap showed how average addiction level changed across screen time and a second selected variable. A scatterplot showed individual users by addiction level and a custom Risk Score Index. A radar chart compared behavioural profiles between addicted and non-addicted users. The goal was to make the dataset easier to explore without reducing it to a single chart or one fixed interpretation. This project was done in the Data Visualization module at the University of St Andrews.

The Challenge

The main challenge was turning a dataset with several behavioural indicators into a visualisation that was clear enough to read, but still detailed enough to explore. Early sketches tested different directions, including density views, correlation matrices, ridgeline plots, bubble charts and a risk score index. Some ideas were useful, but they also made the dashboard harder to follow or placed too much emphasis on one variable.

We had to balance overview and detail. The heatmap made broad patterns visible, but grouped individual users into bins. The scatterplot showed variation within addiction categories, but needed jitter and filtering to reduce overlap. The radar chart helped compare behavioural profiles, but was less precise for exact values. The final design used these limits as part of the structure: each chart answered a different part of the question instead of trying to explain everything at once.

My Role

My main responsibility was designing and implementing the scatterplot: Risk Score Index by Addiction Level. I created a custom risk score to combine several behavioural variables into one comparable measure, including daily screen time, social media use, notifications, app opens, stress, academic or work impact and sleep. I used min-max normalisation to bring those variables onto the same scale and convert them into a score from 0 to 100.

I also worked on the interaction and usability of the view. This included the gender filter, age range slider, tooltips, jitter, layout adjustments and styling refinements. During the build, I had to keep testing small design decisions because changes in spacing, filtering or encoding could affect the readability of the full dashboard. The process helped me understand D3 as both a coding tool and a design material: every visual choice had to work in the data, the interface and the browser.

Design Exploration

Before building the dashboard, we explored different ways of making smartphone usage patterns visible. The early sketches tested density-based views, subgroup comparisons, risk score approaches, correlation views and charts that combined screen time, sleep and stress. Some directions were useful but too visually dense. Others focused too strongly on one relationship, such as screen time and sleep, and left other behavioural indicators in the background. This helped us move towards a dashboard structure where each view had a specific role and avoid trying to make one chart explain the whole dataset.

Visualisation Strategy

The final dashboard was organised around three complementary views. The heatmap provided a broad overview of how average addiction level changed across screen time and another selected variable. The scatterplot made individual users visible through addiction level and the Risk Score Index. The radar chart compared behavioural profiles between addicted and non-addicted users. This structure gave each chart a clear purpose. The heatmap was useful for spotting broader patterns, the scatterplot helped reveal variation within addiction categories, and the radar chart made behavioural profiles easier to compare.

Building the Risk Score Index

For the scatterplot, I created a Risk Score Index to compare users across several behavioural indicators. Instead of relying on one variable, the score combined daily screen time, social media use, notifications, app opens, stress, academic or work impact and sleep. To make these variables comparable, I normalised them and converted the result into a 0–100 score. This made it possible to position individual users on a shared scale while still keeping addiction level visible as a category.

Key Insights

The final dashboard made several patterns easier to see. In the scatterplot, users in the severe addiction category tended to appear at higher Risk Score values, while non-addicted and mild users were more concentrated at lower values. The radar chart showed stronger differences in daily screen time and social media use between addicted and non-addicted users. The heatmap also showed a consistent pattern: as screen time increased, average addiction level tended to rise, especially when higher screen time appeared together with lower sleep duration.

What I Learned

This project helped me understand how closely design and implementation are connected in data visualisation. A chart could look clear in a sketch, but become harder to read once real data, filters and browser behaviour were involved. It also helped me become more comfortable with D3.js as a design tool. I had to think about visual encoding, interaction, layout and data processing at the same time, and make trade-offs when an idea was too complex for the time and scope of the project.
